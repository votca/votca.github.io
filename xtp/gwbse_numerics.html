4. GW-BSE in Practice: Methods, Numerics and Settings¶

Electronic Structure Theories introduces the quasiparticle (QP) equation and the Bethe-Salpeter equation (BSE) [Hedin:1965] [Hybertsen:1986] [Rohlfing:2000]. This chapter describes how VOTCA-XTP solves them: which levels enter where, how the self-energy is evaluated, how the QP equation is solved for each level, how the eigenvalue self-consistency (evGW) is iterated, and how the BSE is set up and solved. It also explains the numerical difficulties one runs into in practice – several solutions of the QP equation, levels that hop between them, self-consistency loops that stall – and what the settings do about them. All options mentioned below are in the gwbse section of the options file; their defaults are those of the current version.

4.1. Overview of a GW-BSE calculation¶

A dftgwbse or QM/MM GW-BSE job runs through the following steps:

  1. Level windows. From the DFT orbitals, three ranges of molecular orbitals (MOs) are chosen: the levels that enter the screening (RPA window), the levels for which QP energies are computed (GW window) and the levels from which the electron-hole pairs of the BSE are built (BSE window). See Level windows.

  2. Three-centre integrals. With the resolution of identity (RI) in the auxiliary basis, the Coulomb interaction between orbital products is expressed by three-centre integrals \(M^P_{mn}\), with \(m\) in the GW (or BSE) window, \(n\) in the RPA window and \(P\) an auxiliary function, in an orthonormalised auxiliary basis in which the bare Coulomb interaction is \(v_{mn,kl}=\sum_P M^P_{mn}M^P_{kl}\).

  3. Exchange self-energy \(\Sigma_\mathrm{x}\), computed once.

  4. Screening. The screened interaction \(W\) from the random-phase approximation (RPA), either through a plasmon-pole model (PPM) or exactly through the RPA excitation spectrum. See The correlation self-energy.

  5. QP equation for every level of the GW window, see Solving the quasiparticle equation.

  6. Self-consistency (evGW): the QP energies replace the DFT energies in \(G\) and \(W\), and steps 4 and 5 are repeated until the energies no longer change, see Eigenvalue self-consistency (evGW).

  7. QP Hamiltonian. The off-diagonal elements of \(\Sigma\) give the QP Hamiltonian \(H^\mathrm{QP}\) and, by diagonalising it, the “diagonalized” QP energies (DQP) next to the perturbative ones (PQP).

  8. BSE with static screening for singlets and/or triplets, optionally followed by a perturbative correction for dynamical screening, see Bethe-Salpeter equation.

The log file reports each step with time stamps. At the default log level (--verbose1 for more, -v for debug output) it contains the level ranges, a summary of every screening build (number of active PPM modes, or RPA correlation energy and lowest RPA poles for exact screening), the evGW iteration summary, the QP and BSE energies and a timing summary of the GW part.

4.2. Level windows¶

4.2.1. Ranges by index¶

The option ranges sets the windows by orbital index:

default

all levels in the RPA; GW and BSE from the lowest level up to \(3\,\mathrm{HOMO}+1\), i.e. twice as many virtual as occupied levels;

factor

rpamax, qpmin, qpmax, bsemin, bsemax as fractions of the number of levels or of occupied levels;

explicit

the same five values as orbital indices;

full

all levels everywhere.

The default choice is generous. For larger molecules its upper end lies 20 eV or more above the LUMO (C60: level 538, about 24 eV above the LUMO; a D5M dimer: level 994). These levels are expensive – every QP iteration solves the QP equation for each of them – and they lie in the energy range where the QP picture becomes ambiguous (see Levels in the satellite region).

4.2.2. Energy caps¶

The options qp_emax, qp_emin, bse_emax and bse_emin (in eV; 0 means off, the default) shrink the windows set by ranges to the levels within the given energy above the DFT LUMO (*_emax) or below the DFT HOMO (*_emin):

<qp_emax>15</qp_emax>
<bse_emax>15</bse_emax>

The caps only ever remove levels. A set of levels within 5·10-4 Ha of the edge level is kept whole, so that a degenerate set is never cut. For open-shell (UKS) references a level is kept if it is within the cap in either spin channel. The log reports every cap, for example GW window top capped by qp_emax = 20 eV: 13 -> 11 (2 levels removed).

Which cap is appropriate is a physical choice. The low-lying excitations of organic molecules are usually built from transitions into virtual levels within 10–15 eV of the LUMO; the trouble with satellites described below typically starts 20 eV or more above it.

4.2.3. Core levels¶

ignore_corelevels removes the chemical core levels (counted per element) from parts of the calculation:

RPA

from the screening, the GW window and the BSE (frozen core);

GW

from the GW window and the BSE, but they still screen;

BSE

from the BSE only;

none

(default) all levels everywhere.

With RPA the three-centre integrals start at the first valence level, so the exchange self-energy would only sum over the occupied valence levels, while \(V_\mathrm{xc}\) contains the core density. core_exchange (default true) adds the missing exchange with the core levels,

\[\Delta\Sigma^\mathrm{x}_{mn} = -\sum_{c\in\mathrm{core}} (mc|cn),\]

computed once from three-centre integrals filled for this purpose. With it, frozen-core GW reproduces all-electron GW closely: for a QM9 molecule (def2-TZVP, 9 core levels, evGW with exact \(\Sigma\)) the exchange self-energies agree to all printed digits, the QP energies within 0.01 eV and the lowest singlet within 3 meV, at 30 % lower cost. Without the core exchange the occupied QP energies are too high by 0.6 eV on average (up to 1.4 eV) and the virtual ones by 0.25 eV. core_exchange=false restores that behaviour for comparison with old results.

4.2.4. Levels outside the GW window¶

Levels in the RPA (or BSE) window but outside the GW window have no QP energy of their own. In evGW their energies in \(G\) and \(W\) are the DFT energies shifted according to out_of_window_shift:

max (default)

by the largest QP correction of the occupied (for levels below the window) or virtual (above) window levels;

nearest

by the correction of the window level next to the boundary;

linear

by a linear fit of the corrections of the window levels next to the boundary (a fraction out_of_window_fit_fraction of them, at least two), extrapolated at most over the energy span of the fit.

The shift is updated in every evGW iteration. The BSE uses the same shifted energies, without off-diagonal QP elements, for virtual levels above the GW window (if bse_emax or bsemax reaches further than qp_emax or qpmax) and occupied levels below it. The further the BSE extends beyond the GW window, the more the choice of shift matters; linear follows the trend of the corrections near the edge.

4.3. The correlation self-energy¶

4.3.1. Pole structure¶

All self-energy models used for the QP equation give the diagonal correlation self-energy of a level \(n\) as a sum over poles,

(4.1)¶\[\begin{split}\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn}(\omega) = \sum_{m}\sum_{s} \frac{a_{nms}\,(\omega - z_{ms})}{(\omega - z_{ms})^2 + \eta^2}, \qquad z_{ms} = \begin{cases} \varepsilon_m - \Omega_s & m\ \text{occupied}\\ \varepsilon_m + \Omega_s & m\ \text{virtual} \end{cases}\end{split}\]

with \(m\) running over the RPA window, \(s\) over the modes of the screening with frequencies \(\Omega_s\), non-negative weights \(a_{nms}\) and the broadening eta (default \(10^{-3}\) Ha). The poles \(z_{ms}\) combine an orbital energy in \(G\) with an excitation energy of the screening. This structure determines how the QP equation behaves (Solving the quasiparticle equation).

4.3.2. Plasmon-pole model (sigma_integrator=ppm, default)¶

The PPM represents the inverse dielectric matrix in the auxiliary basis by one pole per eigenmode, fitted at a real and an imaginary frequency in the spirit of [Godby:1989]. The dielectric matrix is computed in the RPA at two frequencies, \(\omega=0\) and the imaginary frequency \(\omega = 0.5\,i\) Ha. Its eigenvectors at \(\omega=0\) define the modes, their eigenvalues the weights, and the second frequency fixes the pole frequency \(\Omega_s\) of each mode. Modes with weight below \(10^{-5}\) are dropped; modes whose fit gives a negative squared frequency are counted and reported in the log (their absolute value is used). The three-centre integrals are then rotated into the frame of the modes, so that \(a_{nms}\propto w_s\,\Omega_s\,|M^s_{nm}|^2\).

Cost per screening build: two dielectric matrices (\(N_\mathrm{aux}^2 N_\mathrm{occ}N_\mathrm{virt}\)), one eigendecomposition (\(N_\mathrm{aux}^3\)) and the rotation of the integrals (\(N_\mathrm{GW}N_\mathrm{RPA}N_\mathrm{aux}^2\)). For large systems the rotation dominates: for the D5M dimer (3195 orbitals, 7949 auxiliary functions, 995 GW levels) it takes about 600 s per build on 28 cores, of a total of about 900 s.

4.3.3. Exact RPA screening (sigma_integrator=exact)¶

The poles of \(W\) are the RPA excitation energies, obtained from the symmetric pair eigenproblem

\[(A-B)^{1/2}(A+B)(A-B)^{1/2}\,\mathbf{X}_s = \Omega_s^2\,\mathbf{X}_s ,\]

of dimension \(N_\mathrm{occ}N_\mathrm{virt}\) (the number of occupied-virtual pairs in the RPA window), with \(A-B\) diagonal for the RPA. The residues follow from the eigenvectors through the screening modes (\(N^5\) rather than \(N^6\)), and Eq.4.1 holds with \(s\) running over all RPA excitations. The eigendecomposition scales as \((N_\mathrm{occ}N_\mathrm{virt})^3\) and dominates for anything but small molecules (QM9 molecule with def2-TZVP: 7775 pairs, about 17 s per build); it limits the method to molecules of a few dozen atoms. In exchange there is no model error.

4.3.4. Contour deformation (sigma_integrator=cda)¶

The frequency integral of \(G W\) is deformed into residues of the poles of \(G\) enclosed by the contour plus an integral along the imaginary axis, evaluated with a Gauss quadrature (quadrature_scheme, quadrature_order); alpha smooths the residue term. The imaginary-axis and tail terms are computed once per screening build. CDA has no off-diagonal elements of \(\Sigma\).

4.3.5. Batched evaluation and far-pole interpolation¶

The QP search (next section) evaluates \(\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn}\) of one level at many frequencies at once – the 60 or so points of the coarse scan of a window of \(\pm0.75\) Ha. For PPM and exact screening these batches use the pole structure of Eq.4.1: poles far from the frequency range \([c-h, c+h]\) are summed at Chebyshev nodes of the range and interpolated, poles close to it are summed at every frequency. For a pole at distance \(d=u\,h\) from the centre, the kernel is analytic inside the Bernstein ellipse with \(\rho = u+\sqrt{u^2-1}\), and \(n\)-node interpolation converges like \(\rho^{-n}\) [Trefethen:2013]. The poles are grouped by \(u\) (48 nodes for \(2\le u<3\), 32 for \(3\le u<5\), 24 for \(5\le u<10\), 16 beyond), so that the interpolation error stays below \(10^{-20}\) of each pole’s contribution; poles with \(u<2\) are summed directly. The result agrees with the term-by-term sum to rounding (\(10^{-13}\) relative in the unit tests). The split is used from 24 frequencies on. For a dimer-sized system it makes a 63-point batch 2.3–2.7 times faster, since only 4–10 % of the poles are near the window.

Single frequencies (the refinement steps of the root search) and derivatives are summed term by term. Note that near a pole, where \(|\omega-z|\) is of the order of \(\eta\), rounding of \(\omega - z\) alone changes a term by up to \(a\,\epsilon\,|\omega| /\eta^2\); values at the same frequency computed in different ways can therefore differ at the \(10^{-12}\)–\(10^{-11}\) relative level, depending on compiler and vectorisation.

4.3.6. Off-diagonal elements¶

The off-diagonal elements needed for \(H^\mathrm{QP}\) are evaluated as blocked matrix products for all pairs at once (PPM, exact). They are only needed for the diagonalised QP energies and for the BSE with use_Hqp_offdiag; for 995 levels (about \(5\cdot10^5\) pairs) they take of the order of ten minutes with PPM.

4.4. Solving the quasiparticle equation¶

4.4.1. The QP equation and its roots¶

For each level \(n\) of the GW window the perturbative QP energy is a root of

(4.2)¶\[f_n(\omega) = \varepsilon_n^\mathrm{KS} + \Sigma^\mathrm{x}_{nn} - V^\mathrm{xc}_{nn} + \Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn}(\omega) - \omega = 0 ,\]

and its quasiparticle weight (spectral weight, renormalisation factor) is

\[Z_n = \left(1 - \left.\frac{\partial\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn}}{\partial\omega} \right|_{\omega=\varepsilon_n^\mathrm{QP}}\right)^{-1} .\]

Between two neighbouring poles of Eq.4.1 (for \(\eta\to0\)), \(\Sigma^\mathrm{c}\) falls from \(+\infty\) to \(-\infty\), so \(f_n\) decreases monotonically from \(+\infty\) to \(-\infty\): there is exactly one root between any two neighbouring poles. A level therefore has as many roots as there are poles, almost all of them with tiny weight – satellites that sit just next to a pole with a small residue. The root one wants is the quasiparticle root with large \(Z\) (typically 0.7–0.95 for valence levels). Away from the gap, where the poles become dense and some carry large residues (above roughly the lowest plasmon-like excitation energy of the system), a level can have two or more roots of comparable weight. Then the QP picture of that level is ambiguous, and which root is “the” QP energy is a matter of definition (see Levels in the satellite region).

The broadening \(\eta\) makes \(f_n\) smooth: each pole becomes a steep but finite rise through zero with positive slope. These crossings are not QP solutions (they have \(Z<0\)) and are rejected.

4.4.2. qp_solver¶

grid (default)

a search for sign changes of \(f_n\) over a window, refinement of the roots found, and selection of one of them; described in the following;

fixedpoint

Newton iteration from the current energy (qp_sc_max_iter, qp_sc_limit), falling back to the grid search if it does not converge. It finds a root near the start, not necessarily the QP root, and is mainly useful for well-behaved levels.

If no root is found at all, the linearised solution \(\varepsilon_n + Z_n f_n(\varepsilon_n)\) is used and the level is reported in the log.

4.4.3. The grid search¶

For each level the search covers the window \([\omega_0-h, \omega_0+h]\) around the current energy \(\omega_0\) (the DFT energy in the first iteration, the previous QP input afterwards), with qp_full_window_half_width \(h\) (default 0.75 Ha). It proceeds in three stages.

Restricted window first (qp_restrict_search, default true). The QP energy of an occupied level must lie below the Fermi level, that of a virtual level above it. The first search therefore covers only the part of the window below \(-\)qp_zero_margin (occupied levels) or above qp_virtual_min_energy (virtual levels, default \(-0.1\) Ha). If the level itself lies outside these bounds – for example an LUMO pulled below \(-0.1\) Ha by an environment – the bound moves to the HOMO-LUMO midpoint of the current energies. If the restricted search finds no acceptable root, the full window is searched with the dense scan.

Coarse adaptive scan (qp_grid_search_mode=adaptive_with_dense_fallback, default). Starting at the centre (in the first iteration the linearised estimate, if it lies in the window), \(f_n\) is evaluated at shells of width qp_adaptive_shell_width (default 0.025 Ha) outwards on both sides, all points in one batch (The correlation self-energy). In every interval with a sign change, a local scan with 12 sub-steps locates the sign change(s) more precisely; if there are several, the one nearest the centre is refined.

Dense fallback. If the adaptive scan yields no acceptable root, the window is scanned uniformly with spacing qp_dense_spacing (default 0.002 Ha) and every sign change is refined. qp_grid_search_mode=dense uses only this scan, adaptive only the adaptive one.

4.4.4. Refinement and acceptance¶

Each sign-change interval is refined to a root by qp_root_finder:

brent (default)

inverse quadratic interpolation with bisection as safeguard [Brent:1973]; superlinear, a few evaluations per root;

bisection

halving the interval; about \(\log_2(\Delta/\delta)\) evaluations.

Both stop when \(|f_n| <\) qp_sc_limit (default \(10^{-6}\) Ha), or when the interval is narrower than \(\tfrac12 Z_\mathrm{min}\,\) qp_sc_limit. A root is accepted if

  • \(|f_n(\omega)|\le\) qp_sc_limit,

  • \(0.05 \le Z \le 1.5\).

The second stopping criterion guarantees that any root with an acceptable weight also has an acceptable residual: near a root \(|f_n'| = 1/Z \le 1/Z_\mathrm{min}\). (Earlier versions stopped at an interval of qp_sc_limit itself; roots with \(Z\) below about 0.5 could then end with a residual above the limit and were discarded although they had been found – with Brent, which returns an end of the interval rather than its midpoint, more often than with bisection. In a CH4 test case one member of a triply degenerate level ended on another root that way.)

4.4.5. Choosing among several roots¶

If several roots are accepted, one is chosen:

  • by weight (first iteration, and always with qp_root_continuity=false): the largest \(Z - 0.1\,|\omega-\omega_0|\), i.e. the largest weight with a slight preference for roots close to the current energy;

  • by continuity (evGW iterations after the first, with qp_root_continuity=true, the default): the root nearest to the current input energy \(\omega_0\) of the level.

Continuity keeps every level on the branch it started on. This matters for levels with two roots of comparable weight: choosing by weight anew in every iteration lets such a level switch branches whenever a small change of \(G\) or \(W\) tips the balance, and the self-consistency then never settles (Levels in the satellite region). For levels with one dominant root both rules give the same result; on a QM9 molecule the results with and without continuity agree in every printed digit.

Levels whose second-best accepted root has at least half the weight of the chosen one are listed at the end of the GW step as competing roots (chosen and alternative energy with their weights). These are the levels whose QP energy depends on the choice of rule.

4.4.6. Accuracy of the roots and the evGW limit¶

The roots are located to within about \(Z\,\) qp_sc_limit. This error enters the evGW residual (next section), so qp_sc_limit must be clearly smaller than the evGW limit sc_limit. With equal values (both \(10^{-5}\) Ha, the former defaults) the residual of the last iterations is dominated by the rounding of the roots: it jumps between a few discrete values around the limit, and whether a run “converges” depends on that rounding rather than on the fixed point. The default qp_sc_limit is therefore \(10^{-6}\) Ha, a decade below sc_limit. With Brent the cost of the extra digit is small (QM9 molecule, exact \(\Sigma\): QP step 6.5 s of 134 s).

4.4.7. Cost of the QP step¶

Per level and iteration the grid search needs about 60 evaluations for the coarse scan (in one batch) and a few per refined root. The scan dominates. With the batched evaluation, the QP step is a minor part of the cost of an evGW iteration with exact \(\Sigma\), and about 40 s per iteration for the 995 levels of the D5M dimer with PPM. The debug log reports the number of evaluations per iteration (QP diagnostics), the timing summary the total time.

4.5. Eigenvalue self-consistency (evGW)¶

mode=evGW (default) replaces the orbital energies in \(G\) and \(W\) by the QP energies and iterates until they are consistent; mode=G0W0 stops after the first QP solve. Only the energies are updated, the orbitals stay the DFT ones (for orbital self-consistency see Quasiparticle self-consistent GW (QSGW)).

4.5.1. The fixed-point problem¶

Write \(\mathbf{E}\) for the vector of energies of the GW window and \(\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{E}_G,\mathbf{E}_W)\) for the QP energies obtained with the energies \(\mathbf{E}_G\) in \(G\) and \(\mathbf{E}_W\) in \(W\). evGW looks for

\[\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{E},\mathbf{E}) .\]

Levels outside the GW window follow through out_of_window_shift. An iteration costs one QP solve per level and, if \(W\) is rebuilt, one screening build – which is by far the more expensive part (PPM: two dielectric matrices and the rotation of the integrals; exact: the RPA pair eigenproblem).

4.5.2. Convergence criterion¶

An iteration is judged by its QP residual

\[r = \max_n \left| Q_n - E_n^\mathrm{in}\right| ,\]

the largest difference between the QP solutions and the energies they were solved with, i.e. before any mixing. Earlier versions tested the change of the mixed energies between iterations. With Anderson mixing that change can become small while the energies are not a fixed point – when the mixing stagnates or a level alternates between two roots – and runs then stopped at a residual of up to \(10^{-3}\) Ha. The residual test cannot be fooled that way; on the other hand some runs now take a few more iterations.

At the info level (--verbose1) every iteration logs the residual and the five levels with the largest residual as level: input -> solution (residual). This line shows directly what holds a run up: levels that creep towards their solution, levels that jump between two values, or core levels that change after every rebuild of \(W\).

4.5.3. Mixing¶

The input of the next iteration is obtained from the history of inputs and QP solutions by mixing_order:

0

plain update, \(\mathbf{E}^\mathrm{in}\leftarrow\mathbf{Q}\);

1

linear mixing with mixing_alpha (default 0.7);

\(>1\) (default 3)

Anderson mixing [Anderson:1965] with a history of that many previous iterations.

Anderson mixing assumes that the inputs and outputs in its history come from one smooth map. A short history recovers quickly when that is not the case – after a rebuild of \(W\) (the history is restarted then, see below), or when a level switches root. With a long history (the former default 20) a few levels switching between nearby roots locked the mixing on a D5M dimer: it returned the same input for several iterations although the QP solutions were clearly elsewhere. On a QM9 molecule histories of 3, 5 and 20 give identical results and iteration counts.

4.5.4. Adaptive screening update¶

screening_update decides when \(W\) is rebuilt:

every

in every iteration (the classic scheme);

adaptive (default)

\(G\) is iterated with \(W\) fixed, and \(W\) is rebuilt once the energies have settled for that \(W\).

With adaptive, after each QP solve with residual \(r\) the drift \(d = \max_n |E_n - E_n^W|\) of the energies from those \(W\) was built with is compared:

  • \(r <\) sc_limit and \(d <\) sc_limit: converged;

  • \(r <\) sc_limit but \(d \ge\) sc_limit: rebuild \(W\);

  • \(r <\) screening_update_ratio \(\times\, d\) (default 0.1), or screening_update_max_inner (default 10) iterations with the same \(W\): rebuild \(W\);

  • otherwise: next iteration with the same \(W\).

The convergence test requires both a small residual and a \(W\) built from the final energies, so the result is the same evGW fixed point as with every. Only the path differs: \(G\) converges against a frozen \(W\) first, and \(W\) is rebuilt when \(G\) has caught up. The Anderson history is restarted at every rebuild, because the map changes with \(W\).

Typical savings: QM9 molecule (exact \(\Sigma\)) 10 → 6 screening builds, GW time 192 s → 124 s; D5M dimer (PPM, 995 levels) 11 builds for 36 QP iterations, GW 3.4 h instead of 6 h. The summary line evGW: N QP iterations, M screening builds (adaptive) reports both numbers.

Each rebuild of \(W\) moves the energies by a fraction of their drift since the previous build (about 10 % on QM9 molecules, about 30 % on the dimer), so the number of builds grows with the logarithm of the initial drift over sc_limit. Levels that hardly influence \(W\) but react to it – deep core levels in the GW window – can dominate the last builds: on the D5M dimer the last six of eleven builds were spent on five core levels at \(-8.8\) Ha that changed by \(10^{-4}\) Ha after every rebuild, while nothing near the gap moved any more. ignore_corelevels or qp_emin remove such levels from the GW window.

4.5.5. Root tracking (qp_root_tracking, optional)¶

With qp_root_tracking=true the QP search of a level after the first iteration covers only a window around its root of the previous iteration (four times its last change, between 0.01 and 0.1 Ha) and keeps the nearest accepted root, unless its weight has dropped below half of the previous one; otherwise the full search is used. Once converged, one QP solve with the full search checks the roots; if it chooses other roots for some levels, they are logged and the run continues with the full search.

Tracking makes the QP step much cheaper (most levels need only a short local scan) and suppresses root switching after rebuilds of \(W\). It has two drawbacks. The root a level gets now depends on its previous root, not only on the current energies, so the QP map changes from iteration to iteration for levels that sometimes fall back to the full search; Anderson mixing can then stall (seen on the D5M dimer). And where a level has two self-consistent solutions, tracking may end on another one than the full search would. Root continuity (the default) has neither problem: it uses the full window in every iteration but chooses the root nearest the current input, a function of the current energies only.

4.6. Quasiparticle self-consistent GW (QSGW)¶

evGW updates only the energies; the orbitals stay those of the DFT start, and so does the dependence of the result on the functional. QSGW [vanSchilfgaarde:2006] also updates the orbitals: it replaces the exchange-correlation potential by a static, Hermitian approximation of the self-energy and iterates until the orbitals and energies of that Hamiltonian reproduce themselves. do_qsgw=true runs QSGW after the perturbative step (mode=G0W0 or evGW), whose QP energies are the starting point. The QSGW energies replace the perturbative ones, and the BSE is set up in the QSGW orbitals.

QSGW is available for closed shells (RKS) with sigma_integrator=ppm or exact. With cda it stops with an error: the quadrature becomes ill-conditioned as the orbitals rotate.

4.6.1. The QSGW Hamiltonian¶

In the DFT orbitals of the GW window the QSGW Hamiltonian is

(4.3)¶\[H^\mathrm{QSGW} = \operatorname{diag}(\varepsilon^\mathrm{DFT}) - V^\mathrm{xc} - a\,K[\rho^\mathrm{DFT}] + \Delta J + U\,\tilde\Sigma\,U^\mathsf{T} ,\]

with \(U\) the current QP orbitals (columns, expanded in the DFT orbitals) and \(\tilde\Sigma\) the static self-energy in those orbitals. Its eigenvalues are the QP energies, its eigenvectors the new QP orbitals. The terms:

  • \(\tilde\Sigma = \Sigma^\mathrm{x} + \tilde\Sigma^\mathrm{c}\) (+ \(\Sigma^\mathrm{reac}\) in a polarizable environment, see Embedded GW-BSE: Screening by a Polarizable Environment). \(\Sigma^\mathrm{x}\) is the full exchange of the QP orbitals, with the frozen-core exchange if core_exchange=true. \(\tilde\Sigma^\mathrm{c}\) is the static correlation part, see The static self-energy and its regularization.

  • Hybrid starts. \(V^\mathrm{xc}\) holds only the semilocal part of a hybrid functional with exact-exchange fraction \(a\); the term \(-a K[\rho^\mathrm{DFT}]\) removes the hybrid’s exchange (including the frozen core) once, so that QSGW from PBE and from PBE0 solve the same equations. With a frozen core and core_exchange=false the core exchange would stay at \(a\) times its value and the result would depend on the functional; the log warns about it. Range-separated hybrids are refused.

  • Hartree update (qsgw_hartree_update, default true). \(H^\mathrm{KS}\) holds the Hartree potential of the DFT density; \(\Delta J = J[\rho^\mathrm{QP}] - J[\rho^\mathrm{DFT}]\) replaces it by that of the QP density of the active occupied levels (the frozen core does not change). Without it the occupied orbitals rotate in a fixed Hartree potential, which is not self-consistent.

  • Screened Hartree update (qsgw_hartree_screened, default true). In a polarizable environment \(H^\mathrm{KS}\) also holds the potential of the dipoles the DFT density induced. The update then uses \(u = v + v^\mathrm{reac}\), the interaction \(W\) is built from, so the induced dipoles follow the QP density. false keeps the environment’s response to the DFT density, a control: with it the fixed point still depends on the start (6–40 meV median spread for methane in five Thole sites, depending on how far the starts are apart).

4.6.2. The loop¶

Each iteration

  1. rotates the three-centre integrals to the current QP orbitals (both orbital indices) and rebuilds \(W\) with the current QP energies;

  2. evaluates \(\tilde\Sigma\) and \(\Delta J\) in these orbitals and transforms them to the DFT orbitals;

  3. mixes \(\tilde\Sigma\) (the whole matrix) with the history of previous iterations: Anderson mixing over mixing_order previous iterations followed by linear mixing with mixing_alpha; mixing_order=0 is linear mixing alone;

  4. diagonalises Eq.4.3 for the next QP energies and orbitals.

The loop has converged when the self-energy it produces differs from the one it was given by less than qsgw_sc_limit (largest matrix element) and the QP energies change by less than qsgw_sc_limit; the result is then the eigensystem of the produced Hamiltonian. After qsgw_max_iterations without convergence the log warns and the last iterate is kept.

Every iteration logs max|dE_QP| and max|Sigma_out - Sigma_in|. With the Hartree update or an environment a second line gives the largest change of the Hartree potential (bare and environment parts), the change of \(\Sigma^\mathrm{reac}\) for HOMO and LUMO, and the largest rotation between the occupied DFT and QP spaces in degrees.

Virtual levels far from their DFT energy. High virtual levels often have perturbative corrections of several eV and destabilise the loop. Before the loop, the GW window is cut below the first virtual level whose perturbative correction exceeds qsgw_max_virt_correction (default 0.5 Ha); the levels above keep their perturbative energies and DFT orbitals. The log reports the cut.

4.6.3. The static self-energy and its regularization¶

\(\tilde\Sigma^\mathrm{c}\) has to be static and Hermitian, but the correlation self-energy is neither. Off the diagonal it generalises the pole sum Eq.4.1 to

\[\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn'}(\omega) = \sum_{p} \frac{b_{nn'p}\,(\omega - z_{p})}{(\omega - z_{p})^2 + \eta^2}, \qquad b_{nn'p} = c_p\, r_{np}\, r_{n'p} ,\]

with \(p = (m,s)\) running over the poles, \(r_{np}\) the residues and \(c_p\) the mode weights of the self-energy model. Two static forms are implemented (qsgw_regularization).

eta (default)

the symmetrised self-energy at the QP energies,

\[\tilde\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn'} = \tfrac12\left[ \Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn'}(E_n) + \Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn'}(E_{n'})\right] ,\]

with the broadening eta. A pole \(z_p\) close to a QP energy gives a term up to \(1/(2\eta)\) in size, so with the default \(\eta = 10^{-3}\) Ha the Hamiltonian changes abruptly as levels move past poles.

srg

the form of the similarity-renormalization-group derivation of Marie and Loos [Marie:2023] (their eq. 44): with \(\Delta_{np} = E_n - z_p\),

\[\tilde\Sigma^\mathrm{c}_{nn'} = \sum_p b_{nn'p}\, \frac{\Delta_{np} + \Delta_{n'p}}{\Delta_{np}^2 + \Delta_{n'p}^2} \left[1 - e^{-s(\Delta_{np}^2 + \Delta_{n'p}^2)}\right] ,\]

on the diagonal \(b_{nnp}\,(1 - e^{-2s\Delta_{np}^2})/\Delta_{np}\). It is Hermitian without symmetrisation, and every term stays finite: a pole at distance \(\Delta\) from both levels contributes at most \(\sim\sqrt{s}\), and terms with \(|\Delta| \gg s^{-1/2}\) are the undamped \(1/\Delta\). The flow parameter qsgw_srg_s (in Ha-2, default 500) plays the role of \(1/(2\eta^2)\): \(s = 200\) corresponds to \(\eta = 0.05\) Ha, the default 500 to \(\eta \approx 0.032\) Ha. For \(s \to \infty\) the diagonal becomes that of the eta form at \(\eta \to 0\); for \(s \to 0\) the correlation part vanishes (Hartree-Fock). Marie and Loos recommend \(s\) = 500 or 1000; in their molecular test set qsGW did not converge for larger \(s\).

Why the regularization matters. QSGW is a nonlinear fixed-point problem and can have several solutions. With small \(\eta\) it often does: for methane/3-21G with exact \(\Sigma\) and all levels in the window, starts from 20 randomly rotated orbital sets reach another fixed point in 3–10 cases, with HOMO or LUMO up to 0.18 eV apart; 10–25 % of the runs do not converge in 100 iterations, and the others need 45–90. A start-dependent result is then not a property of the method but of the solution the run happened to find. Both regularizations remove this, at a price that differs:

SRG and the eta form are different methods. Off the diagonal the two static operators differ even where no regularization acts: for a pole far from both levels, srg gives \((\Delta_{np} + \Delta_{n'p})/(\Delta_{np}^2 + \Delta_{n'p}^2)\), the eta form \(\tfrac12(1/\Delta_{np} + 1/\Delta_{n'p})\). Marie and Loos find the same direction over 50 molecules: SRG-qsGW ionization potentials are 0.06 eV lower on average than qsGW ones (water: 0.10 eV) and closer to \(\Delta\)CCSD(T). The disabled unit tests qsgw_fixed_point_seed_scan and qsgw_regularization_scan (test_gw) reproduce these numbers; run them with --run_test=gw_test/<name>.

4.6.4. Recommended settings¶

  • Use qsgw_regularization=srg with the default qsgw_srg_s=500, and report \(s\) and the form. To quote a regularization uncertainty, repeat with \(s\) = 200 and 1000. Much larger \(s\) brings back near-singular terms: for water the loop needs 74–172 iterations at \(s = 20000\), and the two starts differ by 1.3–1.5 meV for \(s\) = 5000–20000; Marie and Loos report failed convergence for 11 of 50 molecules at \(s = 5000\).

  • Check that the fixed point does not depend on the start before interpreting a starting-point dependence – for instance QSGW from PBE and from PBE0 should agree to within qsgw_sc_limit.

  • Keep core_exchange=true with a frozen core, in particular from hybrid starts.

  • With environment screening keep qsgw_hartree_screened=true.

  • Keep the default mixing (mixing_order=3, mixing_alpha=0.7). A long Anderson history stalls: for water with srg, order 20 with \(\alpha = 0.2\) left a residual of 0.3 eV after 200 iterations where the default converges in 20.

  • If the loop does not converge, check the virtual cut (qsgw_max_virt_correction) and the mixing first; mixing_order=0 with a small mixing_alpha shows whether the problem is the mixing or the fixed point.

4.7. Levels in the satellite region¶

Far enough above the gap (or below it, for deep occupied levels), the poles of Eq.4.1 become dense and some carry large residues. A level there can have two or more roots of comparable weight, and the QP picture of that level breaks down; multiple solutions of the QP equation and the discontinuities they cause are well documented [vanSetten:2015] [Veril:2018]. Three things can then happen in evGW.

Root switching. If the root is chosen by weight in every iteration, a level with two roots of similar \(Z\) takes whichever is ahead at the moment; small changes of \(G\) or, after a rebuild, of \(W\) move it back and forth. The residual then stays at the distance between the roots. Root continuity (default) prevents this.

Several fixed points. The two roots can each belong to a self-consistent solution: the level is consistent on either branch. Which one a run ends on depends on the path – the root rule, the mixing, even rounding. An example is an open-shell cation in a small basis whose beta level 12 has self-consistent solutions at \(+2.41\) and \(+2.73\) Ha; different settings reached either. Both are valid evGW solutions; the QP energy of such a level is not well defined.

No fixed point. For some levels the root near the input moves away as the input approaches it, because the level’s own energy shifts the poles it sees. A performance run on C60 with the generous default window showed degenerate sets of virtual levels about 24 eV above the LUMO alternating between roots 25–29 mHa apart: from input 0.780 Ha the solution was 0.808 Ha, from input 0.808 Ha it was 0.780 Ha. No setting of the root search can make such a level converge, and the run stops after sc_max_iter iterations with a warning.

The levels concerned are far from the gap. They hardly influence the low-lying excitations, but evGW requires every level of the window to converge, and they cost time in every iteration. The remedy is to keep them out of the GW window (qp_emax, see Level windows); above the window their energies follow out_of_window_shift. The top-5 residual lines and the competing-roots list identify them.

4.8. Bethe-Salpeter equation¶

4.8.1. Hamiltonian and solvers¶

The BSE is built in the product space of the BSE window, with the QP energies of the GW window (diagonal unless use_Hqp_offdiag), the exchange term with the bare Coulomb interaction and the direct term with the static screened interaction \(W(\omega=0)\). exctotal sets the number of states.

With useTDA=true only the resonant block is solved (Tamm-Dancoff approximation). Otherwise the full BSE, whose eigenvalue problem for real orbitals is equivalent to the symmetric problem

\[(A-B)^{1/2}(A+B)(A-B)^{1/2}\,\mathbf{T} = \Omega^2\,\mathbf{T} ,\]

is solved in this Hermitian form by a dedicated Davidson solver; it never forms the dense blocks for the exchange and QP terms. davidson sets the correction method, tolerance, subspace update and iteration limit. The log prints every Davidson iteration with the largest residual and the number of converged roots; the solver stops once the requested roots are converged.

4.8.2. Caches for the direct term¶

The direct term needs \(W(0)\) between electron-electron and hole-hole products. It can be applied from the three-centre integrals in every Davidson iteration or from a precomputed matrix. The precomputed matrices are kept as lower-triangle tiles (half the memory and half the flops to build) and shared between the solves and the analysis of the states. Whether they are kept depends on the memory budget (--memory in GB on the command line); the log reports the decision, e.g. BSE direct terms (lower triangle, 0.0171 GB each): Hd kept; Hd2 kept.

4.8.3. Dynamical screening¶

dyn_screen_max_iter > 0 adds a perturbative correction for the frequency dependence of \(W\) to each BSE state, iterated to dyn_screen_tol. The corrected energies are printed after the static ones (in the order of the static roots).

4.9. Performance and memory¶

4.9.1. Where the time goes¶

The log ends the GW step with a timing summary. Typical shares:

  • PPM, large system (D5M dimer, 995 GW levels, 11 builds): rotation of the integrals into the PPM frame 59 %, dielectric matrices 26 %, QP equations 13 %; off-diagonal \(\Sigma\) another 13 minutes after GW.

  • Exact \(\Sigma\) , small molecule (QM9, def2-TZVP): the RPA pair eigenproblem 88 %, residues 5 %, QP equations 6 %.

The number of screening builds is the main lever; the adaptive update, the default settings and a GW window without satellite-region and deep-core levels reduce it.

For many small molecules (data sets), the dense eigensolvers run far below peak on many threads; running several molecules in parallel with fewer threads each gives more molecules per hour than one molecule on all threads.

4.9.2. Memory¶

--memory (GB) is the budget for choices that trade memory for speed (BSE caches, batch sizes); it does not limit what a calculation needs anyway. The log reports the resident and peak memory after the three-centre integrals and around the first screening build. The three-centre integrals of the GW window dominate for large systems (D5M dimer: about 200 GB). Exact \(\Sigma\) needs the RPA pair matrix (\((N_\mathrm{occ}N_\mathrm{virt})^2\)) and the residues.

4.9.3. Platforms¶

The dense linear algebra uses MKL where available (dsyevd, threaded dsyrk), Apple Accelerate’s LAPACK on macOS 13.3 and later, and Eigen otherwise; the log names the backend. CUDA support has been removed.
